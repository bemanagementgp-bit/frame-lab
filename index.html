How to make a Polaroid-style photo

The look of an instant print comes from its proportions, not from a filter. A classic print is about 88 × 107 mm with a 79 mm square image window, which gives the famous 1:1.2 card ratio, thin side borders and a bottom border roughly four times taller than the top. Frame Lab reproduces those ratios exactly, so your photo reads as a physical object instead of a photo with a white border pasted around it.

  1. Crop for the square. Drag the photo inside the frame until the subject sits in the window, and use the zoom slider to tighten the crop. Faces and single objects work best; wide landscapes lose their horizon.
  2. Write the caption by hand. Keep it short: a place, a date or a private joke. The casual font stack mimics marker on glossy paper; switch to serif for a more editorial look or mono for a lab-print feel.
  3. Tilt it a little. Two to six degrees is enough to suggest a print that was dropped on a desk. More than ten starts to feel like a scrapbook.
  4. Pick a surface. The wall and desk backgrounds add a soft shadow and vignette, while transparent lets you drop the print onto your own design.

Turn on the date stamp for the orange "'26 10 26" mark that film point-and-shoots burned into the corner, and try the black frame with a cream caption for a night-shot look.

Framing tips for prints and galleries

The gallery frame mimics what a framer would build: a moulding, a mat (passe-partout) and glass. A mat of 8–14% of the frame size suits most photos; go wider for small, quiet images and narrower for busy, high-contrast ones. Wood moulding warms up portraits and street photography, black suits black-and-white work and architecture, while white disappears on a white wall and lets the image carry the room. Keep the shadow between 40 and 60 so the frame looks hung rather than floating, and use the Landscape size if you are showing the frame on a wall mockup for an Etsy listing or print shop.

Where to post what you make

Instagram carousels work well with a set of instant prints that share one caption font and background; export each at Square or Portrait so the carousel does not jump in height. Etsy and print-on-demand listings convert better when the first image shows the print framed on a wall, which is exactly what the gallery frame plus the wall background produces. Portfolios and case studies benefit from the laptop and phone frames on a plain gradient, and the film strip is a quick way to present a series of three related shots in one image. Stories and Reels covers should use the Story size so nothing gets cropped by the app.

Your photos stay on your device

Frame Lab is a static page. When you drop or paste a photo it is decoded into memory, drawn onto a canvas together with the frame and background, and encoded back to PNG or JPG by your browser. There is no upload endpoint and no server-side rendering, so family photos, unreleased product shots and client work never leave your computer. Your frame settings are saved in localStorage so the tool opens exactly how you left it.
